# UNO “No Mercy” Comprehensive Rules Guide

UNO Show ‘Em No Mercy (often referred to simply as “UNO No Mercy”) is a variant of the classic UNO card game that introduces new cards and brutal rules on top of standard gameplay 1 2. Below is a detailed breakdown of **official classic UNO rules** and the **special UNO No Mercy rules**, with clear distinctions between them. Examples are provided for clarity, and optional community variations are noted where relevant.

## Standard UNO Rules (Classic Game)

**Objective:** Be the first to get rid of all cards in your hand. In classic UNO, if you win a round, you may score points for opponents’ cards; the first to 500 points wins the match 3 4.

## Setup and Turn Order

*   **Players & Deal:** 2-10 players; each is dealt 7 cards. The remaining cards form a **Draw Pile** (face-down). Flip the top card to start a **Discard Pile** 5 6. If the first flipped card is an Action card (Skip, Reverse, etc.), follow its action (with exceptions for Wild cards as noted below) 7.
*   **Starting Play:** The player to the dealer’s left goes first, and play proceeds clockwise 8. In 2-player UNO, play simply alternates turns (see **Action Cards** below for 2-player effects).

## Gameplay Basics

*   **Matching and Playing Cards:** On your turn, you may play **one** card from your hand that matches either the color or number (or symbol) of the top Discard Pile card 6. *Example:* If the Discard Pile shows a red 8, you can play any red card or any 8 (of any color). You may also play a Wild card on your turn, since wilds are playable on anything 6.
*   **Drawing Cards (Standard Rule):** If you do not have a playable card, you must draw **one card** from the Draw Pile. If the drawn card is playable, you may immediately play it; otherwise, keep it and your turn ends 9. (You may also choose to pass and draw one even if you had a playable card; if the drawn card is playable, you can play it, but you cannot play any other card from your hand in that turn 10 9.)
*   **One Card Per Turn:** In classic UNO you can only play one card on your turn – you **cannot** play multiple cards together. For example, you may **not** throw down two “Draw Two” cards at once or stack a Draw Two on another in the same turn 11 (stacking is a house rule in standard UNO, not official). This “one card only” rule still applies under No Mercy, except where a special card effect explicitly lets you discard extra cards (see **Discard All** below).
*   **UNO Declaration:** When you play your second-to-last card, leaving you with one card, you must immediately yell “UNO!” to declare it. If another player catches you failing to say UNO before the next player begins their turn, you must draw 2 cards as a penalty 3. (If you are not caught in time, no penalty is applied.) *Example:* You play your second-to-last card. If you forget to say “UNO” and

## Standard UNO Card Types and Effects

UNO’s deck consists of four colored suits (red, yellow, green, blue), numbered cards, and five types of action cards. Below are the **classic UNO card functions**:

*   **Skip:** Skips the next player’s turn <sup>12</sup>. The next player is “skipped” and loses their turn. *Example:* You play a Skip card on a blue 5; the next player in clockwise order is skipped and play moves to the following player <sup>12</sup>. (In a 2-player game, a Skip effectively skips your only opponent, giving you an immediate extra turn.)
*   **Reverse:** Reverses the direction of play <sup>13</sup>. If play was moving clockwise, it becomes counter-clockwise, and vice versa. *Example:* In a 4-player game (A→B→C→D clockwise), if C plays a Reverse, play direction flips; now D will play next, then C, B, etc. <sup>13</sup>. (With 2 players, a Reverse acts like a Skip – it flips direction, which in a two-player circle means it immediately comes back to you <sup>14</sup>, effectively skipping the other player.)
*   **Draw Two (+2):** The next player must draw 2 cards and lose their turn <sup>15</sup>. *Example:* You play a yellow “Draw Two”; the next player draws two cards from the pile and is skipped, so the following player plays next <sup>15</sup>. This card can only be played on a matching color or on another Draw Two of any color <sup>15</sup>.
*   **Wild:** The Wild card can be played on **any** color or number, no match needed <sup>16</sup>. The player who plays it chooses the color that play continues with. *Example:* If you have no matching color/number, you can lay down a Wild and declare “green,” so the next player must play a green card (or another Wild) <sup>16</sup>. A Wild can be played even if you had other playable cards (you are not required to hold a Wild for lack of color) <sup>16</sup>.
*   **Wild Draw Four (+4):** This card acts as a Wild (you choose the next color) *and* forces the next player to draw 4 cards and skip their turn <sup>17</sup>. **Restriction:** You should **only play a Wild +4 if you have no card in your hand that matches the current color on the discard pile** <sup>17</sup>. If you hold a matching color but play a +4 anyway, you are playing it “illegally.”
*   **Challenge Rule:** If a Wild +4 is played on you, and you suspect the person had a matching color and played +4 dishonestly, you may **challenge**. The challenged player must reveal their hand <sup>17</sup>:
    *   If they **did** have a card of the required color (i.e. they cheated), the played Wild +4 is forfeited – the cheater must take the 4-card draw penalty themselves <sup>17</sup>, and their turn ends.
    *   If they **did not** have a matching card (the +4 was legal), the challenge backfires: you (the challenger) must draw **6 cards** instead (the original 4 plus a 2-card penalty) <sup>18</sup>.
*   *Example:* The top discard is a red 9. Alice plays a Wild Draw Four and declares “blue.” Bob suspects Alice had a red card. Bob challenges: Alice shows her hand and indeed had a red card. Alice broke the rule, so she must draw the 4 cards instead of Bob <sup>18</sup>. If Alice had no red, Bob’s challenge fails and Bob must draw 6 cards.

## Winning a Round and Scoring (Classic UNO)

A round ends when a player plays their last card. That player wins the round. If you’re playing just a single round, that player is the winner. In multi-round play, you tally points from opponents’ remaining cards. Standard UNO scoring: number cards count their face value, Draw Two/Reverse/Skip count 20 each, Wilds and Wild +4 count 50 each ²¹. The winner of the round collects those points. Rounds repeat until someone reaches (or exceeds) 500 points, and that person wins the game ³ ⁴.

Example: In a 3-player game, you go out. The other two players have cards left: one has a hand totaling 20 points (e.g. two 10’s) and the other 50 points (e.g. a Wild). You score 70 points for that round. If that put you over 500 total, you win the match.

Note: In classic UNO, there is no elimination during the game – all players remain until a round ends. The “No Mercy” variant introduces a knock-out rule described below.

## UNO “No Mercy” Rules (UNO Show ‘Em No Mercy Variant)

UNO No Mercy follows all the base rules of UNO unless stated otherwise, but it ramps up the chaos with additional cards and special rules. It uses an expanded deck (168 cards instead of 108) with new action cards and wild cards ¹ ²². The goal is still to shed all your cards, but in this variant you can also win by **knocking out** all other players ²³.

Key Concept: UNO No Mercy adds **4 major rules** on top of the classic game: Stacking, 7’s Swap, 0’s Pass, and the **Mercy rule (25-card limit)** ²⁴ ²⁵. It also introduces new card types that change gameplay. Below, we explain each new rule in detail, along with the new card functions, and how they modify classic UNO.

### Core No Mercy Rule Modifications

* Draw-To-Match (Continuous Drawing): In UNO No Mercy, the drawing rule is more unforgiving: if you cannot play a card, you must keep drawing **until** you draw a card that is playable ²⁶ ²⁷. The moment you draw a playable card, you *must* play it. This is different from standard UNO where you draw only one card and end your turn if you still can’t play ²⁸.
Example: The discard pile shows a green 5, and you have no green or 5 or wild. You start drawing: you draw one card at a time – if the first card you draw is a green or 5 or wild, you play it immediately and stop drawing ²⁶. If not, you keep drawing until you find a card you can play. This rule can quickly flood unlucky players with many cards, setting the stage for the **Mercy rule** below.

*   **Stacking Draw Cards:** UNO No Mercy *allows and encourages stacking* of draw penalties, a feature not in the classic rules 29 30. If a Draw card is played on you (+2, +4, +6, or +10), you have the chance to “**stack**” by playing your own **Draw card** in response *instead of drawing*. The card you play must be of **equal or greater** value than the one played on you 31 32. By stacking, you pass the cumulative draw penalty to the next player.
    *How it works:* Suppose the player before you plays a Draw Two (+2) card targeting you. In No Mercy, if you have any Draw card:

*   If you play another **Draw Two**, the penalty stacks to +4 total, and the next player must now draw 4 (unless they can stack again) 33 34.
*   If you play a **Wild Draw Four** on a +2, the total becomes +6 to the next player.
*   If you play a **Wild Draw 6** or **Draw 10** (new cards, explained later) on a smaller draw, the total increases accordingly (e.g. +2 -> you play +6, total becomes 8) 32 35.
*   You can even play an equal card to just pass it on: e.g. play a +4 on a +4 to pass along +4 (total stays 4, but now aimed at next player). However, usually one tries to increase or at least not decrease the penalty since it must be equal or higher.

The **next player** then faces the accumulated total. They either absorb it (draw that many cards) **or** if they have a Draw card of value >= **the total**, they can play it to continue the chain 32 36. This chain continues until some player cannot or decides not to stack, and then that unlucky player must draw the full accumulated number of cards and loses their turn 37 36. There is effectively “no mercy” for them – they take the entire hit.

*Example:* Alice plays a Draw 4 on Bob. Bob has a Wild Draw 6 in hand, which is higher, so Bob “stacks” it instead of drawing. Now Charlie (next in turn) faces a looming 10-card draw (4+6). Charlie has no draw card to stack, so Charlie must draw **10 cards** and is skipped 32 38. The chain ends there, and play continues with the next player after Charlie.
*Another example:* Dave plays a Wild Draw 10 on Erin (+10). Erin plays a Draw 10 as well (equal value) to pass it on to Frank – the total is still 10, but now Frank is on the hook. If Frank now plays a Wild Draw 10 too, it passes to the next, still 10. If the next player cannot stack, they draw 10. (Playing equal value doesn’t increase the total, but it does transfer the burden away from yourself.)

**Important:** Stacking in No Mercy applies **only to Draw cards** (cards that force draws). You cannot stack other action cards like Skips or Reverses in the same way. Also, stacking is a response **in turn order** – only the player who is up to act (the one about to suffer the draw) may stack, and they add **one card** to the chain. You still play only one card per turn; you’re just using your turn to continue the stack instead of doing nothing and drawing cards 39.

*   **7’s Swap Rule:** When you play a 7 (of any color), you **must swap your entire hand** with another player of your choice 40 41. This applies to **any** card with the number 7 – including a standard numbered 7 card, or if there were a wild with a 7 (some sources mention a “Wild Draw 7,” but in the official No Mercy deck there is no separate Wild 7 card – only colored 7s trigger this) 42 43. After swapping hands, play continues to the next player in the current direction of play. You essentially give someone your hand and take theirs.
    *Example:* You have just one card left (a risky situation) and you play a blue 7. Per the 7’s Swap rule, you **must** trade hands with any player. You might choose the player who has the fewest cards, giving them your single card and taking their larger hand – effectively transferring the burden to yourself,

*   **0’s Pass (Zero Rule):** When you play a 0 of any color, all players pass their entire hand to the next player in the current direction of play 40 44. This is a rotational hand swap. *   **Mercy Rule (25-Card Knockout):** If at the end of any turn a player has **25 or more cards** in their hand, that player is **knocked out** of the game 45 46. They are eliminated immediately – they do not discard their cards into the pile; instead, their hand is set aside. All their cards are effectively removed from play until the draw pile runs out, at which point those set-aside cards are shuffled to form a new draw pile 45 47. The eliminated player takes no further turns. This rule prevents games from dragging on indefinitely with massive card counts, essentially showing “no mercy” once someone’s hand gets too heavy. Play continues among the remaining players until only one player remains or someone goes out by playing all cards.

    *Example:* After a nasty stacking war, Bob ends his turn with 27 cards in hand. According to the Mercy rule, Bob is knocked out immediately 45 46. Bob’s cards are removed from play (set aside). Now only Alice, Charlie, and Diana remain in the game. If later the Draw pile is exhausted, the set-aside pile (including Bob’s old hand) will be shuffled back in for drawing 47, but Bob is out and can’t win. If all other players eventually get knocked out or one of them empties their hand, the game ends.

**Win Conditions in No Mercy:** There are **two ways** to win a game of UNO No Mercy: (1) play all of your cards (classic UNO win condition), or (2) be the **last player standing** after all others have been knocked out by the Mercy rule 23 48. In other words, you can win by elimination or by going out. As soon as one of these conditions is met, the game ends. If playing multiple rounds for points, you can also score points (see **Scoring** below) – but in many No Mercy games, the emphasis is on survival or emptying your hand rather than long-match scoring.

## New Wild Cards in UNO No Mercy

The No Mercy deck comes with four new types of Wild cards (in addition to the standard **Wild** and **Wild Draw Four** which remain in the deck). As Wilds, each of these can be played on any color, and when you play them you choose the color for play to continue (except as noted). Their added effects make the game more intense:

* **Wild Draw 6 (+6):** This card forces the next player to draw **6 cards** and lose their turn 54. It’s similar to a Wild Draw 4 but with an even larger penalty. There is *no restriction* about needing a certain hand to play it (No Mercy rules do *not mention any challenge* or “must have no color” rule for Wild +6) 55 56 – effectively, you can play a Wild +6 any time as long as it’s your turn. The next player simply suffers the +6 unless they can stack another draw card on top (see Stacking rule). *Example:* You declare “red” with a Wild Draw 6; the next player must draw 6 cards and is skipped, unless they respond with, say, their own Draw 10 to stack (because 10 is higher than 6) 31.

If only two players are in the game, a Wild Reverse Draw 4 will **reverse** to your opponent but with 2 players a Reverse means you’re next again – in this special case the rules say with 2 players, **the card “skips” the other and makes you (the player who played it) draw 4 cards!** <sup>61</sup> <sup>62</sup> In other words, if you use this as your last card in a 1v1 duel, it backfires on you unless you can respond. However, the **Stacking rule** does apply here: even if you would be the one to draw the 4 (in a 2-player scenario), you are allowed to immediately play a Draw card to stack and send the penalty back <sup>61</sup> <sup>63</sup>. So in 1v1, a Wild Reverse +4 essentially initiates a draw-stacking war between the two players until someone can’t continue. In multi-player, it just reverses direction and nails the new next player with +4 like a normal draw effect (that player can try to stack further if they have a +4, +6, +10, etc.).

    *Example (4 players):* Order is Alice → Bob → Charlie → Dana. Alice plays a Wild Reverse Draw 4 and calls “blue.” Immediately the play order reverses (now Alice ← Bob ← Charlie ← Dana). The **new next player** after Alice is Dana (who was previous in counter-clockwise order). So Dana must draw 4 cards and skip their turn <sup>59</sup>. Play continues with Charlie (since direction is now reversed).

    *Example (2 players):* It’s just you and an opponent. You play Wild Reverse Draw 4. Reverse takes effect (which in 2-player acts like a Skip), so technically it would be your turn again – but now the card’s draw 4 effect says the “next” player draws 4, which in a 2-player context is you, the one who played it <sup>62</sup> <sup>64</sup>! You can avoid drawing by immediately stacking a equal/higher Draw card (e.g. play a Wild Draw 4 or Draw 6 from your hand) to bounce it back. If you do, your opponent now faces the accumulated draw. Essentially it becomes a high-stakes ping-pong until someone draws <sup>61</sup> <sup>63</sup>. Use this card with caution as your final move in a duel – if you can’t follow through with another draw card, you’ll end up drawing 4 yourself and prolonging the game.

## Turn Order and Interaction Notes

*   **Direction Changes:** The presence of **Reverse** cards and **Wild Reverse Draw 4** means direction can change often. Remember that a standard Reverse just flips play direction (and acts as a Skip in 2-player), while Wild Reverse Draw 4 flips direction *and then* hits the new next player with a draw. If a Reverse card is played, it also affects who the “next player” is for any pending draw stack. For example, if a Draw 2 was played and then a Reverse is played by the next player (this scenario is a bit unusual because normally one would either draw or stack a draw card, not play a non-draw; but if a Reverse were somehow legally played in response to a draw – say the previous card wasn’t a draw but a normal play then Reverse), it just changes direction of play. Typically, only draw cards can be played to respond to draws in this variant (to stack); a regular Reverse cannot counter a draw card penalty in official rules (some casual play groups allow a Reverse to “reflect” a draw in two-player games, but this is a house rule, not an official No Mercy rule). So under official No Mercy, treat draws and reverses separately: Reverse changes direction, and draw cards pass along penalties. There is **no special “reverse stacking” rule** beyond using the **Wild Reverse +4 card** itself as described.

*   **Skip Everyone & Direction:** The Skip Everyone card doesn’t change direction; it simply skips all opponents. After you play Skip Everyone and take your extra turn, the direction of play remains as it was. So you effectively get two consecutive plays. If your second play after Skip Everyone is a Reverse, then the direction will swap at that point. If it’s another Skip Everyone, you’d skip everyone *again* and still keep the turn! (Though you’d have burned two powerful cards to essentially just chain your turns.)

*   **Interaction of 7 and 0:** If a 0 is played, everyone’s hands move around the table. If a 7 is played after that, only the person who played the 7 swaps with someone of their choice (7 and 0 effects don’t combine or anything – they are separate events). If a 7 is played and the player happens to swap with someone, then a 0 is played by another player right after, that new distribution will include whatever hand-swaps have happened. *Important:* If you play a 7 as your last card, you technically

## Ending the Game and Scoring in No Mercy

A No Mercy game can end by a player emptying their hand **or** by all but one player being knocked out (last survivor). If playing just one game, that’s the end. If playing multiple rounds for an overall score, you can use the point system similar to standard UNO, with a twist for knockouts:

*   **Point Values:** Use the same card values as classic UNO: number cards = face value, Skip/Reverse/Draw2 = 20 each, Wilds (including Wild +4, and all new wilds like +6, +10, etc.) = 50 each 21. The new action cards **Discard All** and **Skip Everyone** count as 20 points each (they are colored action cards) 73, and the new wild cards (Draw 6, Draw 10, Reverse Draw 4, Color Roulette) count as 50 each (wild action cards) 74.
*   **Knockout Bonus:** In UNO No Mercy, if you win a round by playing all your cards, you also get an additional **bonus for any players who were knocked out** earlier in that round: typically **+250 points** for each player that had been eliminated by the Mercy rule 75. (This bonus is applied instead of counting the huge number of cards they had, since their cards were set aside and not in play at round end.) For example, if two players got knocked out during the round, the winner’s score gets an extra 500 points on top of the card values from remaining players’ hands 75 76. If a player somehow knocks *themselves* out (e.g. by drawing past 25 on their own turn), they obviously can’t win, and no bonus is awarded to them. Only surviving players who win a round get knockout bonuses 77.
*   **Winning the Match:** A common target score is 1000 points for UNO No Mercy (since more points can be scored with the bigger deck and knockout bonuses) 78 79. You can agree on a target (500, 1000, etc.) before starting. The first player to reach or exceed that score through accumulated round wins is the champion.

## Popular Variations and House Rules (Optional)

While the rules above cover the official “Show ‘Em No Mercy” game, UNO players often have their own table variations. Here are a few community-adopted tweaks (optional – agree with all players beforehand):

*   **Wild Draw Challenge:** As noted, the official No Mercy rules do *not* mention challenging Wild Draw 4/6/10 plays 55. By default, that means **no challenges** – any Wild Draw played is fair game and cannot be contested. However, some groups choose to extend the classic UNO challenge rule to **all** wild draw cards. In such a variant, if someone plays a Wild +6 or +10 while holding a card of the current color, you could challenge similarly to the Wild +4 rule. If the player was bluffing (had a matching color), they’d draw the cards instead; if not, the challenger draws extra (e.g. 6 or 10 + 2 penalty). This is a **house rule** extension – it’s not in Mattel’s official No Mercy instructions, so use it only if all players agree.

*   **Stacking Other Cards:** The official stacking rule applies only to draw cards 31 32. Some house rules allow **stacking of Skips or Reverses** (e.g. if someone plays a Skip on you, you immediately play your own Skip to “stack” skip the next person, resulting in two people skipped). Another common casual rule is **“Jump-In”**: if you have exactly the same card that’s on top of the discard (same color and number/symbol), you can play it out of turn to jump in. These can make the game even wilder, but **they are not part of official UNO No Mercy rules**. If you want the true “No Mercy” experience as designed, stick to stacking draws only. If you do use jump-ins, remember that a flurry of jump-ins could thwart the 7 or 0 rules (since turn order can get confusing), so use with caution.

*   **Alternate UNO Call Penalty:** Officially, the penalty for failing to say “UNO” is draw 2 3 (and it remains 2 in No Mercy 80). However, many groups play with a stiffer penalty (draw 4) to fit the “No Mercy” spirit. This is entirely optional – just ensure everyone knows which penalty is in effect at the start.

*   **Disabling 7-0 or Mercy:** If you want a slightly less chaotic game, players can agree to **omit the 7 and 0 rules** or the 25-card Mercy rule. These are core to the No Mercy variant, but they could be toggled off as a house preference. For example, you might play with stacking and the new cards, but without hand-swapping on 7s and 0s (some people find constant hand swaps too disruptive). Mattel’s product description even calls the mercy rule an “option” 81, implying you can choose to ignore the elimination aspect if you prefer everyone to play through the whole round.

*   **Clarifying Two-Player Oddities:** In a two-player game, No Mercy is extremely brutal. A common understanding for 2-player is: a **Skip** or **Skip Everyone** card effectively gives the player who played it an immediate extra turn (since there’s only one opponent to skip) 14 49. A **Reverse** card also just gives the same player another turn (reversing a 2-player game just returns turn to self) 14. And as described, **Wild Reverse Draw 4** essentially becomes a self-inflicted +4 unless you can stack back 62 64. Ensure both players understand these interactions to avoid confusion.

The rules listed above for UNO No Mercy are based on the official *UNO Show ‘Em No Mercy* deck released by Mattel in 2023, which encapsulates many “popular” extreme UNO rules in a single game 1 2. Always clarify any house rules before starting, and most of all, **have fun (and show no mercy)!**
